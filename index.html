Core knowledge

What is an enzyme?

An enzyme is a biological catalyst. It speeds up a chemical reaction without being used up or permanently changed by the reaction. Most enzymes are proteins.

Substrate

The substance an enzyme acts on.

Active site

The part of the enzyme where the substrate binds.

Enzyme–substrate complex

Forms temporarily when the substrate binds to the active site.

Products

The substances made by the reaction. They leave the active site.

Diagram 1: Parts of an enzyme

EnzymeSubstrateActive site

The substrate has a complementary shape to the enzyme's active site, allowing it to bind.

Specificity: The active site has a shape that is complementary to its substrate. This means an enzyme usually catalyses only one type of reaction.

How an enzyme works

Follow the sequence from substrate binding to product release.

Step 1

Binding

The substrate collides with and binds to the enzyme's active site.

Step 2

Reaction

An enzyme–substrate complex forms. The enzyme lowers the activation energy needed for the reaction.

Step 3

Release

Products are formed and leave the active site.

Step 4

Reuse

The enzyme is unchanged and can catalyse another reaction.

Diagram 2: The enzyme reaction cycle

The enzyme is not used up. It can bind to another substrate and catalyse another reaction.

Remember: Enzymes do not make reactions happen by supplying energy. They lower the activation energy.

Factors affecting enzyme activity

Diagram 3: Typical patterns in enzyme activity

Temperature: rate rises towards the optimum, then falls steeply as the enzyme denatures.

pH: activity is highest at the enzyme's optimum pH and decreases on either side.

These are schematic curves. The exact optimum and curve shape vary between enzymes.

Temperature

As temperature rises, particles have more kinetic energy. They move faster, so successful collisions between enzyme and substrate become more frequent. The rate increases up to the optimum temperature.

Above the optimum, bonds maintaining the enzyme's shape break. The active site changes shape and the enzyme becomes denatured.

pH

Each enzyme has an optimum pH. Moving away from this pH can alter bonds and charges that maintain the enzyme's shape. The active site changes, so fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form.

At very high or low pH, the enzyme may become denatured.

Substrate concentration

Increasing substrate concentration increases collision frequency and rate, until all active sites are occupied. The enzyme becomes the limiting factor and the rate levels off.

Enzyme concentration

If substrate is plentiful, increasing enzyme concentration provides more active sites, so the rate increases.

Key vocabulary

Optimum: the condition at which an enzyme works at its maximum rate.

Denatured: the enzyme's three-dimensional structure, including its active site, has changed so the substrate no longer fits properly.

Rate of reaction: how quickly reactants are converted into products.

Activation energy: the minimum energy needed for a reaction to occur.

Exam-style written practice

Question: Explain why the rate of an enzyme-controlled reaction increases as temperature rises towards the optimum, but decreases rapidly above the optimum. (4 marks)

Possible 4-mark answer:
  1. As temperature increases, particles gain kinetic energy.
  2. Enzyme and substrate particles move faster, increasing the frequency of successful collisions / enzyme–substrate complex formation.
  3. Above the optimum, bonds maintaining the enzyme's shape break and the active site changes shape.
  4. The enzyme is denatured, so the substrate is no longer complementary to the active site and the rate decreases.
